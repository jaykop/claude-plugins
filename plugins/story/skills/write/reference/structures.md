# Structures that are not a straight line

For stories that split time or viewpoint: the same events seen again through another character, a present-day frame that calls up the past, a mystery in the present that drives a past story, two threads in parallel, frames inside frames. Pick the pattern in step 1; it changes the viewpoint declaration and the step 2 scene list. Sources are in [sources.md](sources.md) under "Structures". Rules marked "estimate" are design choices for games, not findings from the sources.

## What every pattern needs

The pattern lives or dies on one thing: the player always knows which time and whose eyes they are in.

- Design two kinds of transition signal. Main signal: a title card (year and place), a fixed place, a caption. Secondary signal: color tone, the text box frame, the background crop, narration person (first or third), a speaker's way of talking. Never rely on secondary signals alone; many players do not notice them.
- Give each thread or layer one fixed set of signals and never mix them.
- Switch only at scene or section boundaries. One scene holds one viewpoint.
- Add columns to the step 2 scene list: thread or layer, time, viewpoint character, narration person.
- A break in the signal system used exactly once (one scene in another color) adds weight. Used twice, it is noise.
- In a 5-minute scene, use at most two layers (present and past). Three or more layers or two full threads need a story of 30 minutes or more.

Exceptions to step 2:

- "Do not start with a flashback" still holds. A frame starts with a present-day event or question and then calls up the past; that is not a flashback start.
- A scene that revisits events from another viewpoint may link with "그런데 사실은" (but actually) instead of 그리하여 or 하지만. Only at the story level, only for revisits.

## Pattern: viewpoint revisit

The same events seen again through another character.

- How it works: part 1 follows one character (the one being deceived) to the end. Part 2 goes over the same time again through another character (the one deceiving), explaining what was odd in part 1 and adding information part 1 lacked. An optional part 3 is an epilogue or a third viewpoint.
- What the player gets: reinterpretation of scenes already seen, irony, the pleasure of seeing it twice.
- Fairness: the part 1 narrator really did not know. Part 1 contains no false narration and no hidden knowledge; the twist comes from what the part 2 character knew. Read part 1's lines again with part 2's knowledge: none may be a lie. (genre-mystery.md's narrator rule, at the size of a whole structure.)
- A revisited scene adds one new thing. Card fields: same as before / the one thing that changed / how it changes the reading. Two or more changes: split the scene.
- In a reversal scene, write only the surface meaning, fully. The second meaning comes from the next part's context, not from double-meaning lines.
- Inner voice that always tells the truth loses trust at the reversal. Decide the share of lines where inner voice and spoken words diverge (estimate).
- A third part weaker than the first two gets cut down to an epilogue.
- In a VN: the author fixes the order of parts; the player does not choose it, or the twist stops working as "information learned later". Keep one skeleton for the shared events and attach viewpoint lines with conditions rather than copying scenes (estimate). Reuse the same images with a different distance or crop to say "same events, other eyes". Record which parts were seen in a variable.
- Fails when: part 2 repeats part 1 with nothing new; part 1's narrator lies or hides; the last part runs longer than the first two.

## Pattern: called-up frame

A present-day scene calls up the past.

- How it works: the present is a fixed situation (a hearing, a reunion, playing an old video). A present character's question or a prop opens each past scene. Short returns to the present update its state.
- What the player gets: distance (the past is a story someone tells), irony from knowing present results, curiosity about why it is being told now.
- The way in: an entrance prop + an action + a one-line year statement (a box found, play pressed, "그해 우리는 스무 살이었다" in the work's own words). Keep the same entrance every time.
- Before each layer, write: who tells, to whom, why now, and the entrance staging.
- The first present-day scene ends within one group of boxes (about 20–30) and fixes the prop that moves time (estimate).
- Present-day pieces: about 5–10% of the whole, one at the end of each chapter (estimate from one novelization). Each brings a new question, never the past's ending.
- Link past to present with one repeated prop: the object at the end of the past scene is in a hand at the start of the present piece. No explanation needed.
- A sound lead: end a scene with the next scene's question or name being called, and start the next scene with the answer. Time cards at the first one or two jumps only; after that, a question opens the past.
- In a VN: fix the present's background and UI color. Put choices in the present (choosing how to answer). A choice inside the past feels like changing what already happened.
- Fails when: a present piece gives away the past's ending; a present piece has no new information and no question; the present has no title and only a different voice, so players get lost; the entrance prop changes each time.

## Pattern: a mystery in the present

"Which of them is the husband?" — a present-day question that drives the past story.

- How it works: the present shows the answer only as a range of candidates (a fixed few). Each present piece leaks one or two clues (job, where they live, a ring). The past builds an emotional line for each candidate. Late in the story, the past and the present clues meet and settle the answer.
- Clue table: candidates × clues. Each candidate gets at least one clue that rules them out and one that fits. Reveal all clues by the middle. No clue points at one candidate alone.
- If a character has two names (real name, nickname), show both names together once inside a scene, so the player can link them without being lied to.
- Settle the answer inside a scene, in a line between characters, where the past's emotional line pays off; not in a title or a one-line explanation.
- In a VN: the answer is fixed; the player takes part by interpreting, not by choosing. A choice that picks the candidate makes the answer look like the player's doing and weakens the hook. (Choosing a partner by route is a different structure.)
- Five candidates or fewer. An original story introduces the candidates first; it cannot lean on viewers already knowing them.
- Fails when: the clues point at one candidate by the middle; a clue first appears late (unfair); too many candidates for real emotional lines; the answer arrives as a caption.

## Pattern: two threads

Two lines in parallel, often one subjective and one objective.

- How it works: thread A is the protagonist's (first person); thread B is an opponent's or an outside view (third person). Each has its own present and past. Early on, each thread holds a scene for a while; later, scenes shorten and the cuts speed up. At the end, the two endings become each other's reaction: one fact, received differently by two people.
- The blank in three steps: (1) the objective thread shows something happening that cannot be heard; (2) a character in that thread states the blank as a question ("no one knows what they said"); (3) the subjective thread fills it at the end. This keeps a twist fair.
- Narration person splits the threads line by line: first person in A, third person (or no narration) in B. Fix color, speaker tags, and person per thread.
- Choices stay inside one thread; the player does not switch threads by choosing. Allow saving at the end of a scene, not right at a thread switch (estimate).
- Fails when: one thread runs so long the other is forgotten (3 or more scenes in a row, unless speeding up on purpose); both threads repeat the same information; color or person wavers; one scene mixes viewpoints.

## Pattern: frames inside frames

Layer after layer, each told to someone.

- How it works: from the outside in (a reader → an old writer → the writer young → the person who lived it → the main story), and closed in reverse order at the end. The outermost layer closes with a gesture (closing the book, turning off the light).
- Design table per layer: number, period, narrator, listener, why they tell it, entrance staging, exit staging, main signal, secondary signal. Each layer changes one secondary signal (in a VN: the text box frame, the background crop, color tone) and keeps the rest.
- Each layer needs some fun of its own, or the player is bored before reaching the center.
- In a VN: store the current layer in a variable and restore its UI on load (estimate). Two layers in a short work; three or more only in a long one.
- Fails when: only secondary signals mark the layers; exits skip straight to the outermost layer; the player cannot tell which layer they are in.

## Device: the narrator's omission

- How it works: the narrator rushes past the most painful point in a few sentences and changes the subject. A cheerful narration can be a buffer that keeps the listener from the dark part. A prop or the setting hints at the blank.
- Omission is not lying. The narrator never states something false.
- In a VN: at the most important event, the inner voice suddenly shortens or stops. A choice can press on what the narrator avoids; succeeding reveals one line.
- Check: what does this narrator avoid on purpose? Does the avoidance fit their character (a habit, not chance)? Which prop or staging points at the blank?

## Device: a line or object that changes owner

- How it works: a line or prop first appears as one character's weapon or means of control; later another character takes the same words or object and turns the meaning around.
- Show the moment it passes to the new owner (heard, handed over) on screen at least once. Without it, the turn reads as cheating; the screenwriter of the source work called one such case a cheat.
- In a VN: track where the line is used. A choice can ask whether to take up the words. Record it in a variable.
- Fails when: the handover is missing; it repeats so often it reads as a gag; the meaning never actually flips.
- Related: a change of register (polite ↔ casual, dialect ↔ standard) can signal a mask or the true self. Write such a rule on the scene card.

## Check (step 5 pass A)

1. Can the player tell, at every box, which time and whose eyes? Is there a main signal, not only secondary ones?
2. Does each thread or layer keep one set of signals?
3. Does every revisit bring exactly one new thing, and are part 1's lines still true with part 2's knowledge?
4. Does every present-day piece bring a new question without giving away the past's ending?
5. Does every candidate in a present-day mystery have a ruling-out and a fitting clue before the middle?
6. Do the endings of two threads answer each other?
7. Do the layers close in reverse order?
8. Is every changed-owner line shown changing hands?
9. Can the reason for this order be said in one sentence?
