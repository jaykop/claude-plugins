# Cliché check

Used in step 5 pass D. A cliché is something to judge, not something banned. Like genre conventions, repetition itself is not the problem; putting it in because it was easy, with no reason, is. Sources are in [sources.md](sources.md) under "Clichés". The list was chosen for VN mysteries and Korean-set dramas; add rows for other genres from the genre files.

## Procedure

1. Scan the list and mark the lines in the draft that match. Record "none" too.
2. For each marked item ask: (a) was it put in because the story needs it, or because it was easy to write? (b) does it have specifics only this character, this case has? (c) is there a twist or a reason? (d) if you wrote one alternative, what would it be?
3. Judge each: keep, change, or cut. If you keep it, leave an intent comment in the script.
4. If one scene has 3 or more clichés, redesign the scene. Convenience devices have become its skeleton.
5. Do not change a cliché the user put in on purpose. Report it only as "check intent".

When intended use is fine:

1. The pleasure of keeping the genre's promise is the goal, and the expectation is met or twisted on purpose.
2. The character's established personality makes it plausible.
3. The twist has a reason.
4. The character knows the act is strange, or has a motive and situation for it.
5. It is a joke or parody that knows the form.

## List

Form: name — check question / when it is fine.

1. The secret only I don't know (birth secret) — is the only reason the protagonist does not know "everyone hid it"? Can they find it out themselves? / When it is mystery material the protagonist actively chases, or dramatic irony only the player knows.
2. Amnesia, blackout — is there a reason for the memory loss beyond plot convenience? Is the return of memory the result of deduction or choice? / When the gap in memory is itself the clue system and recovering it is the result of the player's actions.
3. Terminal illness — is the illness what changes the character's choices, or a tear-jerking device? / When it ties directly to the theme and the symptoms and timeline are concrete.
4. Approaching under a hidden identity — is the cost of exposure designed? / When the moment of exposure gives the player irony.
5. Overhearing half and misunderstanding — would the same conflict arise if they heard everything? Does the delayed explanation stay credible? / When it brings out distrust that was already in the relationship. Only once.
6. The talkative villain — does the villain have a reason (vanity, need for recognition) to explain the plan? / When the culprit wants their work judged and the protagonist is that audience.
7. Instant confession at the evidence — is the evidence really decisive? Did the culprit get at least one chance to rebut? / When the culprit's guilt built up earlier and they collapse after one rebuttal.
8. Dependence on the twist — does the story stand without the twist? Does the foreshadowing make the twist inevitable? / When the foreshadowing means something different on a second look and connects to the theme.
9. "It was a person, not a ghost" — did a rational explanation erase every bit of the supernatural? / As the base structure of a twisted folktale, leaving one unexplained piece on purpose ([genre-mystery.md](genre-mystery.md) "Supernatural elements and a single truth").
10. The secret passage is the answer — was the building's layout given fairly? / When the layout was explained earlier and the trick combines that information.
11. The evidence was gone when we went back — is how it vanished paid off logically later? / When the removal itself is a clue to the culprit's action and can be deduced from the time gap.
12. The blurry-screen flashback entry — is the flashback really needed? Does the entry signal rely only on convention? / When the flashback shows distorted memory or false testimony as a clue.
13. Hard-boiled monologue — does the monologue line carry only mood, with no information or action? Do the metaphors come from the character's job and era? / When it carries that genre's aesthetic on purpose with controlled length.
14. Rootless period speech — do you know it is conventional speech, not historical accuracy? Is the difference in speech by rank consistent? / When the mood of a folktale setting is the goal and it was simplified for readability. Faction: [genre-faction.md](genre-faction.md).
15. Repeated jargon — does the concept get across without the term? / When the term shows the character's work habit and its meaning comes across through action at first use.
16. Opening setting dump — do 3 or more boxes of setting explanation run in a row? / When the setting rides on a fight or an interrogation.
17. Moving speech, long last words, moral lines — does the scene's emotion get across without it? Does another character's one-line reaction already say it? / When it has a persuasive goal, an audience reaction, and the conflict continues.
18. The genius psychopath with no reason, the evil twin — does the culprit have a concrete motive and a weakness? / When the abnormality ties to the motive and a social problem.
19. The protagonist nobody believes — is the doubter's rebuttal really logical, or were they made stupid for the plot? / When persuasion proceeds by evidence (pointing out contradictions in testimony).
20. Orphan or absent parents as a sympathy device — does the background affect later choices? / When the lack creates a goal and drives the plot.
21. Climax at a ceremony (wedding, ancestral rite, funeral, 60th birthday) — is there a reason for the ceremony beyond gathering the cast? / When the ceremony calls everyone's rank and secrets to one place and each has a goal.
22. The sudden savior — does the protagonist (the player) strike the last blow? Was the savior set up earlier? / When the savior was planted well and the protagonist pays the cost of the rescue.
23. Reconciliation with no trigger — is the trigger visible as an event or action? / When a shallow reconciliation is shown on purpose and breaks again later.
24. Staring at the distant mountain, the same pose repeated — are emotional scenes handled with the same pose again and again? / When the pose change is tied to a story event.
25. A cliffhanger at the end of every scene — did cutting on a crisis every time dull the player? Does the crisis vanish too easily in the next scene's first line? / When resolving the crisis creates a cost in the next scene.

Note: Claude's drafts are expected to lean on clichés that make information easy to deliver (6, 7, 16, 17) and clichés that shortcut emotion (3, 17, 24). This is an estimate, not from the books.
