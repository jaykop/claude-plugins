# Genre: Faction (historical fiction)

For stories set in real history: period dramas (사극), modern-history events, and real people. Read it from step 1. Sources are in [sources.md](sources.md) under "Genres".

## The genre's promise

- What it promises the player: the big course and outcome of history stay as they were, and fictional people or the gaps in the record carry the story.
- Success: the player feels "this could have happened" and can still tell where the fiction is.
- Changing the big outcome makes it alternate history, not faction. That is a different genre choice; agree on it with the user.

Genre formula in six cells (a design aid):

| Cell | Faction |
|---|---|
| Characters | Real people and fictional people |
| Setting | The period |
| Conventions | Fiction placed beside or inside the gaps of real events |
| Conflict | The flow of history against a person |
| Resolution | A personal ending inside the known outcome |
| Value proved | What that period means now |

Reader expectations by subgenre:

| Subgenre | Expectation |
|---|---|
| Faction mystery | A case in a gap of the record, a fictional detective. Borrows mystery technique the most |
| Faction romance | The period's limits (rank, norms) are the obstacle. Also eases the tension of the main line |
| Real-person drama | A known ending, with motives and inner life read anew |
| Modern-event drama | The victim–perpetrator structure kept, a stand-in character for the audience, a notice of what is fiction |
| Alternate history | The big outcome changes. A separate genre; agree with the user first |

## Seven kinds of change

Write a reason for every change. A change made only for convenience, not for mood or theme, is a cut candidate.

1. Inserting a fictional person into a real event (often a stand-in for ordinary people).
2. Merging or modeling: one real person renamed as the lead, or several real people folded into one.
3. Giving a motive the record does not have.
4. Compressing or reordering time (two real visits become one journey; an event moved earlier).
5. Making a villain, simplifying good and evil.
6. Filling a gap in the record.
7. Wish fulfillment: a reward history never gave (punishing someone history let go).

## Boundary rules

1. Do not change the big outcome: who won, who died, the change of power.
2. A real person's motives and character can be new, but must not contradict their recorded actions.
3. Put fictional people beside events or in the gaps. Real people make the central decisions; fictional people witness, influence, or sacrifice.
4. For a modern event with victims, keep the victim–perpetrator structure. A fictional person must not take over a perpetrator's guilt or dilute the harm.
5. When a real person is the model, state the scope of the fiction (a notice at the start).
6. The more recent the event, the narrower the fiction: victims and perpetrators may still be alive.
7. With a known person or ending, put the tension in the process, not the result: who feared what, and how they got through it.
8. Check that objects, groups, and jobs existed then. Mark a deliberate anachronism used for mood as such.
9. Take facts from primary sources (annals, newspapers of the time), not from popular retellings, which change things for fun.

## In a VN

- Choices decide whom to help and what to say inside the outcome, not the outcome itself. If a choice must change the big outcome, the genre becomes alternate history.
- Variables for a real person (trust, affection) move only within their recorded actions. No route has a real person die or survive against the record.
- Split endings by the fictional characters' personal fates (survival, sacrifice, withdrawal). History's outcome is the same in every ending.
- Do not unload the fact list as explanation. Put it in contexts where characters argue (genre-mystery.md, supernatural rule 8).
- Faction mystery: use genre-mystery.md's clue cards and fairness rules as they are. The fact list is background knowledge, not hidden meaning; keep it apart from solution clues.

## Checklist (step 5 pass A and D)

1. Is every item in the fact list marked confirmed, unconfirmed, or deliberate change?
2. Is the big outcome the same in every route and ending?
3. Does every change have a reason, and were convenience-only changes cut?
4. Do fictional people stay out of the central decisions?
5. For an event with victims, is the perpetrator structure kept and the scope of the fiction stated?
6. Does the villain have a motive (not just flat evil)?
7. Can "why this period now" be said in one line?
8. Is the fact explanation not packed into the first few boxes?
9. Do the objects, words, and loanwords fit the period? (Rootless period speech: cliche-check.md 14.)
